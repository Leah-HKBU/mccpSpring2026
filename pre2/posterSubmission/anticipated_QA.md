

## Anticipated Q&A

**Q1. What exactly do you mean by "serendipity" in a recommender system?**
- Serendipity recommendation that is a pleasant surprise to the user, something the user did not expect but ended up enjoying.
- It is distinct from accuracy and novelty. Serendipity combines unexpectedness with relevance.
- It matters because purely accurate recommenders often produce "filter bubbles"; serendipity is a key driver of long-term user satisfaction.

**Q2. Why is serendipity hard to evaluate?**
- It is inherently subjective, whether something is a "pleasant surprise" depends on the individual user's tastes and prior exposure.
- The concept itself is ambiguous: there is no single agreed-upon mathematical definition.
- The two existing options each have a major drawback:
  - User studies are reliable but costly and slow.
  - Proxy metrics are cheap and fast but often misalign with what real users actually perceive as serendipitous.


**Q3. How does SerenEva work end-to-end?**
- Inputs: user-study datasets containing real user serendipity ratings (from the movie and shopping domains), plus user data and item data.
- Process: prompts are constructed from user profiles and item attributes, and an LLM is asked to simulate the user and predict a serendipity rating for each recommended item.
- Outputs: predicted serendipity ratings.
- Meta-evaluation: predictions are compared against real user ratings using Pearson correlation, MAE, and RMSE.

**Q4. Which datasets did you use, and why these two?**
- Serendipity-2018 — a movie-domain dataset.
- Taobao Serendipity — an e-commerce / shopping dataset.
- Both come from real user studies, so the ground-truth ratings reflect actual human perceptions rather than algorithmic proxies.
- Using two domains lets the work test whether findings generalize beyond a single use case.

**Q5. Why use Pearson correlation and MAE/RMSE?**
- Pearson correlation captures whether the LLM ranks items in the same order as the human users — important for recommendation, where ranking matters more than absolute values.
- MAE / RMSE measure absolute prediction error on the rating scale, capturing calibration as well as ranking.
- Reporting both gives a fuller picture: an evaluator could rank well but be poorly calibrated, or vice versa.


**Q6. What did you compare in RQ1?**
- Four method families on both datasets:
  - Proxy metrics — SOG, PURS, DESR, SNPR.
  - LLM-based baselines — LLM4Seren, SerenPrompt_1, SerenPrompt_2.
  - LLM-based inference, zero-shot (ZS) — LLaMA2-7B/13B, Qwen2.5-7B/14B/72B, GPT-4.
  - LLM-based inference, few-shot (FS) — same model lineup as ZS.


**Q7. Why do the smaller LLaMA-2 models perform so poorly?**
- LLaMA2-7B and 13B sit near zero on both datasets in zero-shot, and only marginally improve with few-shot.
- The likely explanation: serendipity judgement requires substantial world knowledge, taste modeling, and instruction following capabilities that scale strongly with model size.
- Larger and more recent models (Qwen2.5-72B, GPT-4) show that the approach is viable, but model capacity is a real constraint.

Q8. Is the LLM advantage consistent across both domains?
- Yes — the same ordering (Proxy < LLM baseline < ZS LLMs < FS LLMs) holds on both Taobao and Serendipity-2018.
- Absolute numbers differ (Serendipity-2018 generally yields higher correlations), but the qualitative conclusion is robust.


**Q9. What auxiliary data did you test?**
- User-side:
  - Long- and short-term profile — generated by an LLM from the user's long-term and recent interaction history.
  - Curiosity — measured using the CEI-II (Curiosity and Exploration Inventory II) psychometric scale.
- Item-side:
  - Popularity — how popular the item is overall.
  - Similarity — how similar the item is to other items (a proxy for unexpectedness).

**Q10. Why is the impact domain-specific?**

- In shopping, purchases are often driven by immediate context and personality traits, so recent behavior and a curiosity score capture surprise well.
- In movies, taste is more stable over time, and surprise is more about how an item relates to a user's broader catalog, so long-term profile and item-to-item similarity matter more.
- This suggests that picking the right auxiliary signals should be informed by the domain, not applied uniformly.



**Q11. What are the main limitations?**
- Two domains only. Movies and shopping share certain consumer-product characteristics; results may differ for news, music, or social content.
- Snapshot evaluation. The framework predicts ratings for fixed item-user pairs and does not directly capture longitudinal effects (does serendipity now lead to retention later?).
- Model dependence. The strongest results rely on frontier-scale LLMs; small models do not work well.
- Prompt sensitivity. Performance depends on prompt design, which limits reproducibility unless prompts are released in full.

**Q12. What's next?**
- Extend to additional domains (news, music, short-video).
- Study temporal dynamics — does LLM-predicted serendipity correlate with downstream engagement?
- Explore fine-tuning open-weight LLMs on user-study data to close the gap with GPT-4 at lower cost.
- Audit for bias: does the LLM systematically over- or under-predict serendipity for certain user demographics or item categories?
